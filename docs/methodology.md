# Methodological Overview

## Study design

This doctoral research adopts an ecological, longitudinal, and spatial analytical design. It examines whether variation in the supply and territorial distribution of public sports and recreation infrastructure is associated with premature mortality from non-communicable diseases among adults aged 30–69 years in São Paulo.

## Conceptual framework

The study is situated at the intersection of Smart and Sustainable Cities, public administration, urban health, spatial epidemiology, and GeoAI. Urban infrastructure is treated as a potential enabling condition for physical activity and health promotion, while mortality indicators represent population-level health outcomes. Socioeconomic, demographic, territorial, and temporal conditions may confound or modify these relationships.

## Units of analysis

Analyses may be conducted at multiple levels depending on source compatibility:

- municipality-year;
- subprefecture-year;
- district-year;
- geographic zone-year.

The finest common spatial and temporal resolution will be preferred, provided that denominator quality, confidentiality, and statistical stability are preserved.

## Study period

The principal analytical period is 2015–2025. Historical data from 2008 onward may support interrupted-period, pre/post, or sensitivity analyses. The years 2020 and 2021 may be excluded in selected models to evaluate the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic, but such exclusion must be justified and reported rather than applied automatically.

## Main constructs

- **Outcome:** premature mortality from selected NCDs among people aged 30–69 years
- **Primary exposure:** availability or density of sports and recreation infrastructure
- **Denominator:** population corresponding to the relevant age, sex, year, and territory
- **Contextual covariates:** territorial vulnerability, socioeconomic conditions, demographic composition, green areas, cycling infrastructure, and other urban characteristics when available

## Analytical principles

1. Preserve the original source files and provenance metadata.
2. Harmonize territorial identifiers before data integration.
3. Report counts together with population-standardized rates.
4. Evaluate missingness, suppressed values, and boundary changes.
5. Assess temporal trend, spatial dependence, and model assumptions.
6. Quantify uncertainty with confidence intervals and sensitivity analyses.
7. Interpret associations without inferring individual-level causality from ecological data.
